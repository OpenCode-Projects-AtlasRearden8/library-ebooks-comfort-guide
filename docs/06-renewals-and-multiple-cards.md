# Renewals and Multiple Cards

Two small skills separate "I occasionally get a book" from "my reading runs like clockwork": handling renewals without stress, and stacking a couple of legitimate library cards. Neither is fancy. Both save real weeks of waiting per year.

## Renewals without drama

A renewal extends your loan without giving the copy back. Rules are simple and un-glamorous:

- You can usually renew from **Shelf → Loans → the book → Renew** (sometimes under a "Manage loan" label).
- If your library and the title's publisher allow it and *nobody is waiting behind you*, you'll typically get another full loan period from today. (Some systems just extend by a fixed block; some cap total renewals — usually one or two.)
- If someone *is* waiting (there's a hold queue), renewal is disabled. That's not punitive; it's the line protecting itself. Cancel-nothing: the copy you hold is owed back on schedule.

If Renew isn't offered:

1. **Check why first.** A hold behind you, a cap reached, or the title simply doesn't allow it (rare for older licenses).
2. **If a queue exists:** ride it out. Finish early, note your page/position (Libby remembers), and — when the title is available again — borrow fresh; your reading position often reappears, which is a kindness the system-dark-ages version of libraries never had.
3. **If returns puzzles you** (loan already "expired" but you were three chapters from the end): re-borrow. If it's suddenly unavailable, that's the queue — chapter 05's options apply.

A calm pattern for *any* long book: borrow at 21 days, renew once mid-book if available, and if renewal was refused, treat the last stretch as a *soft deadline* — finish or re-borrow, nothing terrible either way.

## Early returns: the quiet superpower

Returning before the due date costs nothing and helps the queue (chapter 05). It also has a self-serving bonus on hoopla: **returning an instant borrow early frees your monthly allowance slot**, so you can spend the borrow on your *next* book instead of waiting for the calendar. If you abandoned a title that turned out not to be for you, returning it is both manners and allowance management.

## Multiple cards, used well

Here's the legitimate, surprisingly-common setup: most people are actually eligible for **more than one** library card, and Libby happily holds several at once.

Ways you can be eligible:

- **Your home card** — where you live.
- **A neighbouring county/town's system** where you work, own property, or where your library has a reciprocal agreement. Municipal and county borders are arbitrary w.r.t. what you'll actually read; two nearby systems usually have *different* queues for the same bestseller.
- **A statewide system**, in states that run one.
- **A big-city non-resident card.** Several large public libraries let outsiders sign up — some free, some for a modest yearly fee (that fee goes to the library, not to any guide or app; nobody should be selling you "access" at a markup). Which cities, what fees, and how to sign up is maintained in our companion directory, *Free Reading for Less* — it's a directory question, and directories keep that fresher than books can.

> **What we won't do:** suggest inventing an address. It's fraud (libraries do check), it's unfair to the town, and it's genuinely unnecessary — enough legal paths exist that the shortcut buys you nothing but risk. Everything in this guide works within the rules, which turn out to be pretty wide.

## Setting the cards up in Libby

Adding a card happens where you'd expect:

1. **☰ Menu → Settings → Library Cards** (wording shifts slightly across versions; look for "cards" or "libraries").
2. **Add Another Library** → pick the *new* library → enter that system's card number and PIN.
3. Repeat for as many cards as you actually have.

Then:

- When *browsing*, Libby can search one library or all your libraries at once (a filter toggle). Results show which library has what.
- A title that's available on *one* of your cards is borrowable on that card right there.
- Same title, waitlisted everywhere? Book it everywhere you can — two queues mean two chances, and cancelling an extra hold later costs nothing.
- Each library keeps its **own loans and hold limits** (10 loans on one, 5 on another). The mix is a bit of a spreadsheet, but the app does the math per-card and displays it.

hoopla is per-card in the same way (one account per card), but its allowances start over per *account* — a second eligible library means a second monthly set of instant borrows.

## Keeping the juggling honest and sane

- **Keep each card number + PIN in your password manager**, labelled with the library's name. (Library cards next to each other in a list, otherwise three numbers look identical.)
- **Default card:** pick a primary for browsing (usually your home system — it keeps librarians' local stats honest and queues local). Add the other cards when you want *that specific book sooner*.
- **Return loans on all cards, not just the current one.** Expired loans don't charge you late fees; they just vanish. But each system's *holds* are per-card, and a hold that arrives under card #2 while you're buried will lapse silently — set reminders or unsuspend anything you're not ready to claim yet.
- **One hold per format per card** is usually unnecessary: for most people the multiformat trick (chapter 05) plus one backup card covers realistic needs.

## What to do when a card expires mid-read

Libraries require periodic re-verification (residency proof or just a click). If your loan dies because your card expired:

1. Renew the card via the library's own site (usually a "renew library card" form) or by visiting/contacting the branch.
2. Re-open Libby; the account re-validates.
3. Re-borrow. Your reading position is remembered by both Libby and Kindle for the loan you'd held, so even a gap rarely costs you your page.

One more mindset thing, because it's the actual habit that makes digital borrowing feel abundant: **hold everything interesting the moment you hear about it, borrow whatever's available now, return freely, renew rarely.** The queue system rewards the casual but consistent borrower far more than the optimiser.
