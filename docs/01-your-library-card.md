# Your Library Card: Get One, Use It, Keep It Safe

The card is the smallest piece of this whole system — a number, sometimes a PIN, occasionally a plastic card if you like the old way. But it's also the key to everything else, so it deserves one careful chapter. By the end you'll have a working card (or a plan to get one), you'll know what your PIN is for, and you'll know exactly where those details should and shouldn't live.

## What a library card actually is

A library card is a borrowing account with your local (or chosen) public library. Historically it meant "you can take a paper book home." Today it means the same thing plus a digital layer: ebooks, audiobooks, digital magazines, sometimes comics, music, and even video — all licensed by the library for its cardholders' use.

When you borrow a digital title, you're not downloading a file you own. You're issued a *loan* — a license to read or listen for a set period (usually 7, 14, or 21 days), after which the title checks itself back in. No late fees exist on digital loans. Ever. They simply expire.

## The two things every card has

- **Card number** — a string of digits (usually around 14, but length varies) that identifies your account. It varies from system to system. Some newer cards skip plastic entirely.
- **PIN** — a short code, often 4 digits, that proves it's you. Libraries use different names: PIN, passcode, password, PIN. They're all "the second field on the sign-in screen."

Together they're the login for Libby, hoopla, your library's website, the online catalog, and usually the library's app. They are, taken together, mildly sensitive: someone with your number and PIN could place holds and read books on your dime. Nothing sinister — no money ever moves — but it's *your* account and your waitlist positions.

> **Keep these two safe:** store the number and PIN in a password manager (or in the phone's own "Passwords" app), or write them in the same place you keep other important numbers. Don't leave the pair scrawled on a sticky note on the device itself, and don't share the combination with someone you wouldn't share your email password with.

## The four paths to getting a card

Pick whichever fits your situation:

### 1. In person — the traditional way

Walk into your local branch with a photo ID and something showing your current address (a piece of mail, a lease, an insurance letter). Fill out a short form. You'll typically walk out with your number and PIN in about ten minutes. Some libraries issue cards to kids on a parent's ID; you'll usually fill in the kid's details as a "juvenile card" tied to your custody of the account.

### 2. Online — the increasingly common way

Most libraries let you apply on their website. Search for your library plus "get a library card" or "card application". You'll type your name, address, and contact info; the library checks you against its records; then you receive your new number (often instantly, sometimes by email within a day or two, sometimes by slower mail with the plastic card). If you realize mid-year you're unsure whether you've got the card number or its PIN, this is the usual result of an online application.

### 3. Instant e-card / digital access card

Many libraries now offer an "e-card" or "digital access card": sign up online, and you get number-and-PIN access to digital material right away, with the option to upgrade to a full card later by visiting a branch or completing an identity check. Some larger city libraries sell a *digital access card* — a paid (often low-cost) card for people who can't otherwise be the library's main patrons. That cost, when there is one, is set by and paid to the library; no guide (or app) should mark it up. Whether a non-resident option exists for your city — and any small fee — is catalogued card-by-card in our companion directory, *Free Reading for Less*.

### 4. Statewide or regional systems

A few states run one system for the whole state — apply in one library and your card works across a network. Rural libraries sometimes partner into a regional "consortium." If your own town is small, or the build-your-own-card path feels sparse, ask the library whether your state has a statewide card program. Your eligibility (residency, age, school status) controls what each system will grant you. There's no workaround for "you're not a resident," but there's almost always a legitimate second path.

## Trying your card before you trust it

The fastest test of a card: open Libby (chapter 02), tap **I Have a Library Card**, and enter the number and PIN. If it signs in, your card works for digital material. If it doesn't, one of three things is happening, all of them fixable:

- **Wrong PIN.** Many systems default to the last four digits of your phone number; others use your birth year; others use a random code printed on your signup papers. Try the obvious ones, then reset it on the library's website or by phone.
- **Card expired or never activated.** Cards lapse if the library requires periodic re-verification (often yearly). Some need a quick reactivation at a branch or online.
- **Right number, wrong library.** If your town's library belongs to a regional network, your card works on that network's sign-in portal — not on the town-you-used-to-live-in one. Make sure Libby is pointed at the right system (it searches by name or city, and you can add more later).

> **Why didn't anyone warn me the PIN might be "defaulted"?** Because it surprises everyone. If you can't sign in with your card number, the PIN — not the number — is usually what's wrong.

## Keeping the whole family covered

One card per person is how libraries prefer it (each member of the family can have their own account, even kids as young as they'll let you sign up). Two reasons people often miss:

- **Separate holds.** If you and your teenager both want the same new bestseller, two cards mean two positions in the queue — instead of one shared position and a fight.
- **KIDS mode.** The hoopla app (chapter 03) lets you switch into a children-only experience on a whim, but a genuinely separate kid's card is cleaner: their loans, their queue, their reading history.

If your card number someday says **"invalid"** and you know it expired, library renewal is usually quick: sign in on the library site, answer a residency check, and you're back. (For several states, an annual re-verification of residency is all that's required.)

## Where card details should and shouldn't be

A small, dull-but-valuable habit:

- **Safe places:** a password manager, a notes app that syncs under your own encryption, or a printed page locked in the same drawer as the passports.
- **Not great:** a sticky note with both on it, taped under your laptop; a photo of both on a device other people borrow.
- **Definitely not:** email. Email is a terrible place for any credential — forward, copy, search, sync — treat your card plus PIN the same way you'd treat a password, not the same way you'd treat a receipt.

## When your library has little or no digital collection

If your town library uses none of the big platforms, don't stop here. Nearby county systems, state networks, and several large-city libraries accept non-residents (some free, some for a modest yearly fee). The quick checks:

1. Ask your library direct: have they joined a state/regional digital consortium?
2. Ask whether you qualify for a card from a neighbouring county under a "reciprocal borrowing" agreement between your library and theirs.
3. Check *Free Reading for Less*, our companion directory of genuinely free reading resources, for the public-domain and open-textbook options that need no card at all.
